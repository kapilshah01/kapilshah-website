## Introduction

Passwords still protect many of the services a small business depends on: email, cloud storage, accounting, payroll, customer systems, websites, and payment tools. A password policy gives the business a shared, practical way to create, protect, and recover those passwords. It is not a document designed to make work harder. It is a set of habits that reduces avoidable account risk.

The strongest policy is one people can follow during a busy day. Modern guidance focuses less on complicated character tricks and more on long unique passwords, blocking known weak passwords, password managers, and multi-factor authentication (MFA). [NIST’s Cybersecurity Basics guidance](https://www.nist.gov/itl/smallbusinesscyber/cybersecurity-basics) recommends strong passwords, password managers, and MFA as foundational actions for small businesses.

## What Is a Password Policy?

A password policy is a short set of rules for business accounts. It explains what people should do when they create a password, how they should store it, when they must report a problem, and who manages access when a role changes.

For a small business, the policy does not need to be a long legal document. It should answer ordinary questions clearly: Can I reuse a password? Where do I store one? Can I share an account? What should I do if I clicked a suspicious link? Who removes access when someone leaves?

Your policy should fit the systems, data, contracts, and risks of your organization. This article provides general security guidance, not a substitute for sector-specific legal, contractual, or compliance requirements.

## Why Password Policies Matter for Small Businesses

Without an agreed process, people often reuse passwords, save them in messages or spreadsheets, share an owner login, or leave former workers with access. Those shortcuts can turn one account issue into a wider business problem.

For example, a reused password exposed through an unrelated service can be tried against business email or cloud storage. A shared administrator password makes it difficult to know who has access or remove that access when a contractor leaves. A practical policy gives employees a safe alternative before they need to improvise.

Passwords are only one layer. MFA can require a second sign-in check, helping make a password alone less useful to someone else. Read [Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) for Small Businesses](/guides/mfa-for-small-businesses) for a practical rollout guide.

### 1. Use long passwords or passphrases

Length is more useful than arbitrary complexity rules. A passphrase is a longer password made from several words that is easier to remember when a password manager is not being used. Do not build one from easily guessed business details, names, or predictable phrases.

NIST SP 800-63B-4 requires a minimum of 15 characters for passwords used as a **single authentication factor** in the systems covered by that guidance. It also permits a shorter minimum for passwords used only as part of MFA. This does not mean every application everywhere universally enforces exactly 15 characters; each service has its own settings and requirements. As a small-business policy, choose a long-password baseline that the systems you use can support, encourage passphrases, and allow long generated passwords. [NIST SP 800-63B-4](https://pages.nist.gov/800-63-4/sp800-63b.html) also recommends allowing at least 64 characters and spaces.

### 2. Never reuse a password

Every business account should have its own password. Reuse lets one exposed password affect multiple services, including services that have nothing to do with the original incident.

Do not make exceptions for “low-value” tools that can access business email, customer data, shared files, or password-reset messages. A password manager makes unique passwords realistic without asking people to memorize dozens of secrets.

### 3. Use an approved password manager

A password manager generates and stores long, unique passwords and reduces the urge to write them down or send them in messages. Choose one that supports individual accounts, access management, recovery, and MFA for the manager itself. Follow our [small business password manager guide](/guides/small-business-password-manager-guide) for practical setup steps, vault organization, and offboarding workflows.

The business should define who administers the password-manager account, how emergency access works, and how a departing worker’s shared access is removed. NIST notes that password managers can make it easier to use distinct passwords and supports autofill and paste functionality that lets them work properly.

Set clear boundaries for the tool. Personal vaults should not be the only place a business credential exists, and a shared vault should not grant every employee access to every secret. Start by storing the accounts people need for their work, assign ownership to critical entries, and use the manager’s sharing controls instead of copying a password into a message.

### 4. Enable MFA wherever available

MFA adds another verification step beyond the password. Require it first for business email, password managers, administrators, financial services, domain and hosting accounts, and other systems that could disrupt the business.

Do not approve an MFA prompt you did not initiate or share a verification code with someone who contacts you unexpectedly. MFA is valuable, but it does not replace phishing awareness or unique passwords.

### 5. Block common and compromised passwords

If your identity provider, password manager, or application supports it, block passwords that are common, predictable, or known to have appeared in breaches. This protects people from choosing a password attackers are likely to try first.

SP 800-63B-4 requires the systems it governs to compare a proposed password against a blocklist of commonly used, expected, or compromised passwords. The guidance also says the entire password should be checked and that people should receive a useful reason if their choice is rejected. For small businesses, enable the setting where your provider offers it; do not try to maintain a breach-password list manually.

### 6. Avoid arbitrary periodic password changes

“Change every password every 30 days” is not a good default rule. Frequent forced changes can encourage small, predictable edits and make people more likely to forget or record passwords insecurely.

NIST SP 800-63B-4 says verifiers should not require periodic changes, but should force a change when there is evidence the authenticator has been compromised. Your policy should therefore require a password change when compromise is suspected or confirmed, when a provider requires it after a security event, or when an administrator directs it as part of incident response.

### 7. Never share passwords

Do not send passwords through email, chat, tickets, or text messages. Do not give everyone the same login just because it is convenient. Shared credentials make offboarding, troubleshooting, and accountability difficult.

Use individual accounts and role-based access when a service supports them. When a shared secret is unavoidable for a legacy service, keep it in the approved password manager, limit access to named people, record the owner, and replace it when a person with access leaves or the secret may be exposed.

### 8. Remove access when employees leave

Offboarding is part of password security. When an employee, contractor, or agency relationship ends, remove or disable their accounts, password-manager access, MFA factors, shared mailbox access, and administrator privileges promptly. Follow our comprehensive [employee offboarding security checklist](/guides/employee-offboarding-security-checklist) to coordinate access revocation, data retention, and secrets rotation.

Maintain a simple account inventory so this is manageable. Include business email, cloud tools, website and hosting accounts, finance systems, devices, and any vendor portals. Transfer ownership before removing a sole administrator.

### 9. Protect administrator and privileged accounts

Administrator accounts can create users, change billing, reset passwords, alter website content, or access sensitive data. Give this access only to people who need it and use a separate standard account for daily work where a service supports it.

Require MFA, unique passwords, controlled recovery methods, and regular access review for these accounts. Record who can recover the account if the primary administrator is unavailable.

Keep an emergency record of the business-controlled recovery path without placing recovery codes in an ordinary shared file. A small team should be able to answer: which accounts are administrators, who owns them, what they control, and how the business regains access if one person is unavailable. Test this documentation during a routine review rather than an outage.

### 10. Make the policy practical and understandable

Security rules fail when they are hard to use or unclear. Write the policy in plain language, explain where staff get help, and provide the approved tools. Introduce it during onboarding, review it when systems change, and make reporting a possible mistake easy and blame-free.

The goal is consistent behavior, not catching someone out. If a rule causes repeated workarounds, find the underlying reason and improve the process.

## A Ready-to-Use Small Business Password Policy Starter Template

Adapt this starter policy to your organization and have appropriate advisors review it when you have specific contractual or regulated obligations.

- Every employee must use a unique password for each business account.
- Passwords must be long and should use a passphrase or a password generated by the organization’s approved password manager.
- Passwords must not be shared or sent through email, chat, text, or other insecure channels.
- Employees should use the organization’s approved password manager where practical.
- MFA must be enabled on accounts that support it, starting with high-impact accounts.
- Common, predictable, or known-compromised passwords must not be used where the service can block them.
- Suspected password compromise must be reported immediately through the business’s designated contact route.
- Passwords must be changed when compromise is suspected or confirmed, not simply on an arbitrary recurring schedule.
- Access must be removed or transferred promptly when an employee, contractor, or vendor relationship ends.
- Administrative accounts must have stronger controls, including MFA, named owners, and limited access.

Add the owner, reporting contact, approved password manager, and review date before distributing this policy. A policy with no person responsible for it is unlikely to stay current.

## Small Business Password Security Checklist

- [ ] A short written password policy has a named owner and review date.
- [ ] Every business account uses a unique password.
- [ ] Long passwords, passphrases, or generated passwords are supported and encouraged.
- [ ] An approved password manager is available and protected with MFA.
- [ ] Business email, financial, administrator, domain, and hosting accounts use MFA.
- [ ] High-impact accounts have documented recovery methods and backup access.
- [ ] Common or compromised-password blocking is enabled where available.
- [ ] Passwords are not shared through email, chat, or documents.
- [ ] Offboarding includes account, MFA-factor, and password-manager access removal.
- [ ] Administrator access is limited, reviewed, and separate from ordinary work where possible.

For a broader set of foundational controls, use the [Small Business Cybersecurity Checklist](/guides/small-business-cybersecurity-checklist).

## Common Password Policy Mistakes

- Requiring a complex-looking pattern but allowing short or reused passwords.
- Making people rotate passwords on a schedule without a suspected compromise.
- Providing no password manager, then expecting people to remember many unique passwords.
- Treating MFA as optional for email or administrators.
- Leaving an old contractor as an administrator because nobody knows how to transfer ownership.
- Writing a policy that says “strong password” without explaining what people must actually do.
- Forgetting recovery codes and backup MFA methods until a phone is lost.

## What to Do If a Password Is Suspected to Be Compromised

Report it promptly using the business’s incident contact process. From a known-safe device, change the affected password to a new unique password; if it was reused, change it everywhere it was used. Review account recovery methods, active sessions, forwarding rules, and connected applications. Do not approve unexpected MFA prompts.

Contact the relevant provider or IT support for critical accounts. If a financial account, customer information, or administrator account may be involved, escalate quickly and preserve relevant messages or timestamps. [Small Business Phishing Protection](/guides/small-business-phishing-protection) explains safe handling when a suspicious email or sign-in page may be involved.

## Final Takeaway

A small business does not need a complicated password program. It needs a usable standard: long unique passwords, an approved password manager, MFA, sensible recovery, quick reporting, and prompt access removal. Start with your most important accounts, document who owns them, and improve the policy as your people and systems change.
