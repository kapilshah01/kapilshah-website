## The short answer

A small business incident response plan should tell people how to report a suspected incident, who can make decisions, which outside providers to contact, what evidence to preserve, and how to restore essential work. Keep the plan short, available when business email is unavailable, and review it when people or services change.

### 1. Name the response lead and backup

Choose who coordinates the response and who acts if that person is unavailable. Record who can authorize actions that interrupt service, contact customers, or approve emergency spending. Staff should know that reporting a concern is more important than proving it is an attack.

### 2. Add an offline contact list

Record current contact paths for IT support, email and cloud providers, website and domain hosts, bank or payment providers, insurer, legal counsel if applicable, and relevant business leaders. Keep a copy in a protected location that does not depend on access to the affected account.

### 3. Give staff a clear reporting path

Tell people where to report a suspicious sign-in, lost device, unexpected payment change, malware alert, or exposed information. Include an alternate phone or contact method. Ask them to note what happened and when, and not to delete suspicious messages or approve unexpected sign-in prompts.

### 4. List critical systems and recovery priorities

Identify essential email, customer, financial, file, website, and operational services; their owners; and how to reach the provider. Link to the business's backup and account recovery instructions without placing passwords or recovery codes in the plan itself.

### 5. Set evidence and communication expectations

Preserve relevant messages, timestamps, alerts, screenshots, and provider notices when safe to do so. Assign one person to coordinate internal updates. Do not speculate publicly or send sensitive incident details through an account that may be compromised. Legal, contractual, insurance, and notification duties depend on the facts and jurisdiction; seek qualified advice promptly where they may apply.

## First actions depend on the incident

- **Suspected account takeover:** Use a trusted device and the provider's recovery process; review sessions and recovery methods with support. Do not rely on the potentially compromised inbox for coordination.
- **Lost or stolen device:** Contact the service or device administrator to revoke access where appropriate, initiate remote wipe if enabled, and assess what data the device could reach (see our [work computer and laptop security guide](/guides/small-business-computer-laptop-security) for device encryption and lost-device protocols).
- **Unexpected payment request:** Pause the transfer and verify the request using a known, separate contact method. If an unauthorized transfer has already occurred or an invoice scam is suspected, follow our [business email compromise and payment fraud prevention guide](/guides/business-email-compromise-small-business-payment-fraud) for immediate bank recall and containment steps.
- **Malware or ransomware warning:** Contact IT support, preserve the alert, and follow the response lead's instructions. Avoid improvising a restore while affected systems may still be compromised. Review the [backup and recovery guide](/guides/small-business-backup-ransomware-protection) during planning so the recovery process is understood before an incident.
- **Possible data exposure:** Limit further sharing when safe, preserve records, and quickly involve the appropriate provider and professional advisers.

These are preparation prompts, not a substitute for incident-specific professional response. The safest technical action can vary with the system and evidence that needs preserving.

## Practice the plan

Once or twice a year, walk through a simple scenario without changing production systems. For example, ask how the team would contact the response lead if business email were unavailable, who could reach the domain provider, and where recovery instructions are stored. Update gaps and record an owner for each follow-up.

## Common planning mistakes

- Keeping the only plan in an account that could be locked or compromised.
- Listing a single responder with no backup.
- Storing passwords, MFA seeds, or recovery codes in an ordinary document.
- Assuming staff know where to report an issue.
- Making promises about restoration time without a tested recovery process.
- Treating every incident as identical or delaying specialist help during a serious event.

## Conclusion

An incident plan is useful when a stressed person can find it and act on it. Name a lead and backup, keep essential contacts offline, explain how to report problems, and practice the recovery path before it is needed.
