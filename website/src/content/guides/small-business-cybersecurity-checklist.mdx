## Introduction

Cybersecurity is not only an IT problem. It is part of keeping a small business able to take payments, serve customers, protect information, and keep working after a mistake or disruption. You do not need a large security department to make meaningful progress. You do need to protect the accounts, email, devices, data, and recovery options your business relies on.

This checklist focuses on practical risk reduction, not perfection. The [NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 Small Business Quick-Start Guide](https://www.nist.gov/publications/nist-cybersecurity-framework-20-small-business-quick-start-guide) is designed for organizations with modest or no formal cybersecurity plan; its core idea is to understand what matters to your business, prioritize it, and improve over time. Use the list below as a working plan, adapting it to your systems, contracts, and any legal or regulatory duties that apply to you.

### If you only have 30 minutes today

- Turn on MFA for your business email and the accounts that control money, your domain, or other administrators.
- Make sure important accounts do not share a password, and put new unique passwords in a reputable password manager.
- Tell everyone: verify unexpected payment or account-change requests through a known phone number or separate trusted channel.
- Check that you have a recent backup of the information you would most need tomorrow.

Write down what you complete. A simple list of account owners, backup locations, and next actions is more useful than relying on memory.

You do not have to buy every security product or complete every task at once. Focus first on systems that could stop the business, expose sensitive information, or move money. If a provider manages part of your technology, ask who performs each security task; outsourced technology still needs a clear business owner.

## Accounts and Access

### 1. Turn on MFA for important accounts

MFA (multi-factor authentication) adds another verification step beyond a password. It makes a stolen password less useful on its own. Start with business email, financial services, domain registration, cloud storage, website administration, and any account that can reset other accounts. CISA recommends using MFA wherever possible and, where available, choosing phishing-resistant options. [CISA’s MFA guidance](https://www.cisa.gov/audiences/small-and-medium-businesses/secure-your-business/require-multifactor-authentication) explains the available approaches.

- **What to do:** List these high-impact accounts and enable their strongest practical MFA option. Store backup codes in a protected business-controlled location.
- **Done looks like:** Every active high-impact account that supports MFA requires it, and the person responsible for recovery is known.

### 2. Use unique, strong passwords

One reused password can turn a single account problem into several. Unique passwords contain the damage if a service is breached or a password is mistakenly entered somewhere it should not have been.

- **What to do:** Replace reused passwords on important accounts first. Prefer long, randomly generated passwords when a password manager can store them.
- **Done looks like:** Each important business account has its own password; no password is shared by email, chat, or an unprotected document.

### 3. Use a reputable password manager

A password manager reduces the temptation to reuse passwords and gives a small team a safer way to handle account access. It should not become an informal shared vault with no ownership.

- **What to do:** Choose a well-supported password manager, set up individual accounts where practical, enable MFA on it, and decide who can administer the business account.
- **Done looks like:** The team has an approved place to generate and store passwords, with documented recovery access for the business owner.

### 4. Remove unused accounts

Old staff accounts, former contractors, trial services, and abandoned integrations can remain a path into a business long after they stop being useful.

- **What to do:** Review users in email, cloud storage, payroll, accounting, website, and key software. Disable or delete access that is no longer needed; do not forget shared mailboxes and API or integration accounts.
- **Done looks like:** Every active account has a current business reason and an identifiable owner.

### 5. Review administrator privileges

Administrator accounts can change users, billing, security settings, or an entire service. They deserve closer attention than ordinary day-to-day accounts.

- **What to do:** Identify all administrators. Give admin rights only to people who need them, and use a standard account for routine work when your service supports that separation.
- **Done looks like:** The administrator list is short, reviewed, and documented; administrators use MFA.

## Business Email and Phishing

Phishing is a deceptive email, text, call, or message intended to make someone click, sign in, send money, or reveal information. NIST notes that messages may imitate trusted sources and commonly create urgency. [Its small-business phishing guidance](https://www.nist.gov/itl/smallbusinesscyber/guidance-topic/phishing) recommends pausing and verifying requests through known contact information.

### 6. Secure business email with MFA

Business email often receives password resets, invoices, customer messages, and access notices. If an email account is taken over, it may also be used to reset other services.

- **What to do:** Require MFA for every mailbox, including administrators and shared-mailbox owners. Review forwarding rules and delegated access as part of the setup.
- **Done looks like:** Active email users have MFA, recovery details are business-controlled, and unexpected forwarding is investigated.

### 7. Train people to recognize phishing

People do not need to become investigators. They need a simple habit: pause when a message is unexpected, urgent, or asks them to sign in, open a file, or move money. Examples include a fake invoice, a fake Microsoft or Google sign-in notice, a vendor’s apparent bank-detail change, or an urgent request that appears to come from an executive.

- **What to do:** Explain these examples in plain language and give staff a low-friction way to ask before acting. Repeat the reminder when new people join.
- **Done looks like:** Everyone knows to stop, check, and report suspicious requests without fear of blame.

### 8. Verify unusual payment or account-change requests

Invoice and payment-change fraud works when a business treats an email as proof that a supplier or colleague made a request. A separate verification step can interrupt that pressure.

- **What to do:** Create a rule that bank-detail changes, payment instructions, payroll changes, and unusual purchases must be confirmed using a known phone number or another trusted channel—not details supplied in the message.
- **Done looks like:** The verification rule is written down and the people who approve payments know how to apply it.

### 9. Protect email recovery methods

Recovery email addresses, phone numbers, backup codes, and security questions can be as important as the mailbox password. If they point to a former employee or personal device, recovery may fail when it is most needed.

- **What to do:** Review recovery settings for every administrator and critical mailbox. Move business recovery details under current business control and protect backup codes.
- **Done looks like:** Recovery methods are current, known to authorized decision-makers, and not dependent on a departed person.

### 10. Report suspicious messages instead of interacting with them

Replying, clicking links, downloading files, or using an unsubscribe link in a suspicious message can make a situation worse. Reporting gives the business a chance to warn others and preserve useful details.

- **What to do:** Tell staff to use their email provider’s reporting feature or send the message to the person who manages email security. Do not ask them to forward credentials or open attachments for proof.
- **Done looks like:** The team knows one safe reporting path and understands that reporting quickly is encouraged.

## Computers and Devices

### 11. Keep operating systems updated

Operating-system updates often correct security weaknesses and reliability problems. Delaying every update indefinitely leaves devices without fixes that their maker has already supplied.

- **What to do:** Turn on automatic updates where practical, schedule restarts, and check that updates are actually completing on laptops, desktops, and managed mobile devices.
- **Done looks like:** Supported devices have a named owner and a working update process rather than a collection of overdue prompts.

### 12. Keep applications and browsers updated

Browsers, office software, remote-access tools, accounting applications, and security software also need updates. A fully updated computer can still have an outdated application exposed to everyday work.

- **What to do:** Enable supported automatic updates for core applications and browser extensions. Remove applications that nobody needs instead of carrying their update burden.
- **Done looks like:** Essential software is supported, routinely updated, and unused software has been removed.

### 13. Use supported operating systems

When a manufacturer ends security support, new issues may no longer receive fixes. Continuing to use such a system can be a business decision, but it should be visible and managed rather than accidental.

- **What to do:** Identify unsupported devices and software. Replace, upgrade, or isolate them with help from an IT professional if they are needed for a specialist task.
- **Done looks like:** You have no unknown unsupported systems and a documented plan for any exception.

### 14. Lock devices when unattended

An unlocked laptop or phone can expose email, files, saved browser sessions, and customer information to anyone nearby. This is especially relevant for travel, shared workspaces, and front-desk devices.

- **What to do:** Require a screen lock with a reasonable timeout and a secure sign-in method. Teach staff how to report a lost or stolen device promptly.
- **Done looks like:** Business devices lock automatically, and people know whom to contact if a device is missing.

### 15. Limit unnecessary administrator access

Administrator access on a device can install software and change security settings. Routine work usually does not need that level of control.

- **What to do:** Use standard user accounts for daily work where possible. Keep a separate administrator account for approved maintenance tasks and remove local admin rights that are not needed.
- **Done looks like:** People use elevated access deliberately, not as the default for every task.

## Website and Online Services

### 16. Keep the website, CMS, and plugins updated

Your business may use a content-management system, a hosted website builder, or a custom site. In every case, keep the platform and enabled add-ons supported and maintained; unused plugins and integrations are still things to manage.

- **What to do:** Assign someone to check updates for the website, themes or templates, plugins, hosting control panel, and key integrations. Confirm a backup or export exists before major changes.
- **Done looks like:** Every enabled website component has an owner and a regular update responsibility.

### 17. Protect website administrator accounts with MFA

Website, domain, hosting, and payment administrators can alter public content, change DNS, or disrupt business services. Their protection should match their impact.

- **What to do:** Turn on MFA for these accounts, use separate administrator accounts rather than a shared password where possible, and keep recovery information current.
- **Done looks like:** Critical online-service administrators use unique accounts, MFA, and business-controlled recovery methods.

If a service does not offer MFA, use a unique password, restrict who can administer it, and consider whether a provider with stronger account protection would better fit the importance of that service.

### 18. Remove unused website and service accounts

Former agencies, developers, staff, test accounts, and old integrations can retain access to online services long after a project ends.

- **What to do:** Review user lists at the domain registrar, hosting provider, website platform, payment service, analytics, cloud storage, and other business applications. Disable anything that is no longer required.
- **Done looks like:** Current access is limited to current work, with a named owner for each service.

### 19. Review third-party services and integrations

Small businesses often depend on SaaS platforms, cloud storage, booking tools, payment systems, and connected applications. A useful review asks what each service can access and who is responsible for it.

- **What to do:** Keep a simple inventory: service name, purpose, owner, administrator, data it holds, MFA status, and how access is recovered. Remove connections you no longer use.
- **Done looks like:** You can identify your important services and regain control if a key person is unavailable.

## Business Data and Backups

### 20. Identify your most important business data

Start with the information that would stop work or create the most harm if it were lost, changed, or exposed: customer records, contracts, financial information, email, website content, design files, and essential configurations.

- **What to do:** List the data, where it lives, its owner, and how long the business can operate without it. Include cloud services, local devices, and paper records where relevant.
- **Done looks like:** The business knows what must be protected and restored first.

### 21. Limit access to sensitive information

Not every employee, contractor, or application needs every file. Limiting access by role reduces accidental exposure and narrows the impact if an account is misused.

- **What to do:** Review shared drives, folders, customer systems, and financial applications. Give access based on job need and remove it when the work ends.
- **Done looks like:** Sensitive information is not broadly available just because broad access is convenient.

### 22. Back up important data

Backups help recovery from accidental deletion, hardware failure, service problems, and some security incidents. NIST’s [backup and recovery guide](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/other/2020/04/24/protecting-data-from-ransomware-and-other-data-los/final) emphasizes planning, maintaining, and testing backup files based on each organization’s needs.

- **What to do:** Confirm coverage for critical files, cloud data exports where appropriate, website content, and important system configurations. Choose a frequency that fits how much recent work you could realistically lose.
- **Done looks like:** Critical data has a documented backup method, owner, and review process.

### 23. Keep at least one backup separated from the main system

A backup stored only in the same everyday environment can be affected by the same mistake, outage, or account compromise. Separation might involve a different account, storage location, or provider, depending on your setup.

- **What to do:** Discuss your backup design with your provider or IT support. Protect backup access with strong authentication and make sure the recovery copy is not routinely exposed to every user.
- **Done looks like:** At least one recovery copy is appropriately separated and its access is controlled.

### 24. Test that backups can actually be restored

A successful backup notification does not prove that the needed information can be found, opened, and used. A small restore test reveals missing permissions, incomplete coverage, and unclear instructions before an emergency.

- **What to do:** Restore representative files and, where practical, a critical workflow. Record the date, result, time required, and any issue to fix.
- **Done looks like:** You have completed a recent restore test and know who can perform recovery.

A restore test can begin small: recover one recent file to a safe location, open it, and confirm it is the version you expected. The point is to verify the whole path—backup, access, instructions, and usable data—not to create disruption during a busy workday.

## Incident Response

### 25. Create a simple cybersecurity incident response plan

An incident plan is a short, practical guide for a stressful day. It does not need to be a large policy document. NIST’s small-business resources and CISA’s [small and medium-sized business resources](https://www.cisa.gov/small-and-medium-sized-business-resources) can help you develop it further.

- **What to do:** Name the people who can make decisions; record contacts for IT support, hosting, domain, email, bank or payment providers, insurer, and key vendors; write where account recovery and backup details are stored. Include how staff should report a problem, which evidence to preserve (such as messages, timestamps, and screenshots), and when to request professional help.
- **Done looks like:** Responsible people can find the plan without relying on business email, know who will communicate, and can start recovery without guessing.

## What to Do First

Cybersecurity is continuous improvement. Start with the work that most directly protects access and recovery, then build the habits that keep those controls useful.

### Today

- Enable MFA for business email, financial services, domain or hosting, and administrator accounts.
- Change any reused password on a critical account and begin using a password manager.
- Share the payment-change verification rule with everyone who handles money or supplier details.
- Confirm that a recent backup exists for the data you would miss most tomorrow.

### This Week

- Create the critical-account, service, and data inventories.
- Remove unused users, integrations, local administrator rights, and unneeded software.
- Enable updates and screen locks on supported devices.
- Perform a small restore test and record the result.

### This Month

- Write and share the incident response plan and offline contact list.
- Review website, domain, hosting, and third-party service access.
- Address unsupported devices or software and any gaps found in the backup test.
- Put a recurring review date on the calendar; revisit the checklist when people, suppliers, or systems change.

## When to Get Professional Help

Get qualified IT or cybersecurity help promptly if you suspect an account compromise, malware or ransomware, fraud, lost access to a critical system, exposure of sensitive data, or a major website compromise. Assistance is also sensible when recovery is failing, important services are complex, or your business lacks the expertise to assess a material risk safely.

Preserve what you can without taking risky actions: keep relevant messages, timestamps, screenshots, and provider notices. Contact your bank or payment provider quickly if money or payment details may be involved. A professional can help contain the issue, protect evidence, and plan safe recovery.
