## Introduction

Cybersecurity is part of running a business, but many small businesses do not have a security team to own it full time. That does not mean every owner needs to become a security expert. It means the business needs a practical baseline: protect the accounts, email, devices, data, and recovery options it relies on most.

This checklist is a starting point for reducing avoidable risk. It is not a guarantee that a business will be secure, and it should be adapted to your technology, the data you handle, your contracts, and any legal or regulatory requirements that apply to you. NIST describes cybersecurity as an ongoing risk-management process, not a one-time project. [Its small-business guidance](https://www.nist.gov/itl/smallbusinesscyber/cybersecurity-basics) is a useful companion as your business grows.

Start with the highest-impact actions, document what you have done, and return to the list regularly.

## 1. Secure Your Most Important Accounts

Your email, banking, accounting, website, cloud storage, payroll, and administrator accounts can affect the whole business. Make a short inventory of them first.

- Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) wherever it is available, starting with email and administrator accounts. MFA adds a second check beyond the password; [NIST explains why that extra barrier matters](https://www.nist.gov/itl/smallbusinesscyber/guidance-topic/multi-factor-authentication).
- Use a unique password for every account. A password manager can generate and store them so staff do not need to reuse passwords.
- Remove accounts that are no longer needed, including former staff, old contractors, trial services, and unused integrations.
- Give people only the access they need. Keep administrator access limited to people who genuinely require it.
- Protect account-recovery methods. Review recovery email addresses, phone numbers, backup codes, and who can reset important accounts.

For a focused next step, see [MFA for Small Businesses](/guides/mfa-for-small-businesses).

## 2. Secure Business Email

Email is often where payment requests, password resets, customer messages, and sensitive attachments arrive. Treat it as a business-critical system.

- Require MFA for every business email account.
- Teach staff to pause before opening unexpected links or attachments, particularly when a message creates urgency.
- Verify a payment-change or bank-detail request using a known phone number or a separate, trusted contact method. Do not rely on the contact details in the unexpected message.
- Make sure the people who administer email understand how to recover an account and where recovery details are stored.
- Review shared mailboxes, forwarding rules, and external access regularly.

[CISA's Secure Our World guidance](https://www.cisa.gov/secure-our-world) highlights phishing recognition, strong passwords, MFA, and timely software updates as practical protective habits. For more email-specific guidance, read [Small Business Phishing Protection](/guides/small-business-phishing-protection).

## 3. Secure Computers and Devices

Laptops, phones, tablets, and shared workstations often hold access to customer data and business systems. A lost or unpatched device can become a larger problem if it also has saved sessions or administrator access.

- Turn on automatic updates for operating systems, browsers, business applications, and security software where practical.
- Replace or isolate operating systems and applications that no longer receive security updates.
- Enable device encryption where it is supported and appropriate for the device.
- Require a screen lock with a reasonable timeout, especially on portable and shared devices.
- Remove software, browser extensions, and remote-access tools that are no longer needed.
- Do everyday work with standard accounts where possible; use administrator privileges only when an administrative task requires them.

## 4. Secure Your Website and Online Services

Your public website and cloud services are part of the business perimeter, even if someone else manages them.

- Keep the website platform, themes, plugins, and integrations updated. Remove abandoned or unused plugins rather than leaving them installed.
- Use HTTPS and check that the site redirects visitors to the secure version.
- Review administrator accounts for the website, domain registrar, hosting provider, and cloud services.
- Turn on MFA for services that support it, especially domain, hosting, email, payment, and customer-management accounts.
- Confirm that the website and important online services have backup or export options appropriate to your needs.
- Keep a list of important third-party services, their account owners, and how access can be recovered.

## 5. Protect Business Data

Not every file needs the same protection. Identify the data that would cause the most disruption, privacy harm, contractual concern, or legal issue if it were exposed, changed, or lost.

- List critical data and where it lives: customer records, financial files, contracts, email, source files, website content, and cloud drives.
- Limit access to people who need the data for their job.
- Use approved business storage rather than scattered personal accounts or unmanaged devices.
- Encrypt appropriate portable devices and sensitive data stores when your tools support it.
- Dispose of data and accounts you no longer need according to your business, contractual, and regulatory obligations.

If you handle regulated information, payment data, health information, or customer data under contractual controls, ask qualified legal, privacy, IT, or security professionals what additional requirements apply.

## 6. Back Up Important Data

A backup is useful only if you can find it, access it, and restore what the business needs. NIST's [backup and recovery guidance](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/other/2020/04/24/protecting-data-from-ransomware-and-other-data-los/final) emphasizes planning, maintaining, and testing backups.

- Identify the data, configurations, and systems that are critical to operating the business.
- Use a backup method and frequency that reflect how much data you can afford to lose and how quickly you need to recover.
- Keep at least one backup appropriately separate from the everyday environment, so a problem in the main environment is less likely to affect every copy.
- Check backup jobs for failures.
- Test restoring representative files and, where feasible, a business-critical workflow.
- Write down who can access backups and the first steps for recovery.

## 7. Protect Against Phishing and Social Engineering

Social engineering uses a convincing message, call, or request to pressure someone into acting. The goal may be a password, a payment, sensitive information, or access to a device.

- Treat urgent, unexpected, or secret requests as a reason to slow down.
- Check the full sender address and the real destination of a link before signing in or sending information.
- Do not approve an MFA prompt you did not initiate.
- Use a verification procedure for invoices, payroll changes, gift-card requests, and bank-detail changes.
- Give staff a simple place to report suspicious messages and make it clear that reporting is encouraged.

## 8. Prepare for a Cybersecurity Incident

Preparation reduces confusion. A short response plan is more useful than trying to invent one during an outage or suspected compromise. NIST recommends defined roles, decision-making strategies, tested backups, and an up-to-date contact list as part of recovery planning.

- Name the people who can make business decisions during an incident.
- Keep contact details for your IT provider, hosting provider, domain registrar, bank/payment provider, insurer, and key vendors in a location available if email is unavailable.
- Record how important accounts are recovered and where backup information is held.
- Decide how staff should report a suspected incident and who will communicate with customers or suppliers if needed.
- Preserve relevant messages, timestamps, and screenshots when safe to do so; avoid deleting evidence in a rush.
- Seek qualified help promptly for a suspected compromise involving sensitive data, fraud, ransomware, or systems you cannot safely assess.

## 9. The 25-Point Small Business Cybersecurity Checklist

Work from the top down. “Done” should mean the action has been completed, checked, and recorded—not simply discussed.

1. [ ] **List your critical accounts.** Include email, banking, accounting, payroll, domain, website, cloud storage, and administrator accounts. **Done means:** an owner and recovery method are recorded for each.
2. [ ] **Enable MFA on business email.** Prioritize every mailbox and email administrator. **Done means:** each active account requires a second factor.
3. [ ] **Enable MFA on administrator and financial accounts.** Protect accounts that can change systems or move money. **Done means:** MFA is verified on each account that supports it.
4. [ ] **Use unique passwords.** Stop password reuse across business services. **Done means:** important accounts have distinct, strong passwords.
5. [ ] **Adopt a password manager.** Use it to generate and store passwords instead of sharing them in messages or documents. **Done means:** the team has an approved process for secure password storage.
6. [ ] **Remove unused accounts.** Review former staff, contractors, old tools, and test accounts. **Done means:** unnecessary access is disabled or deleted.
7. [ ] **Limit administrator privileges.** Use admin access only for people and tasks that need it. **Done means:** the current admin list is reviewed and justified.
8. [ ] **Secure account recovery.** Review recovery emails, phone numbers, backup codes, and reset permissions. **Done means:** recovery information is current and controlled.
9. [ ] **Turn on automatic updates.** Cover operating systems, browsers, and core business applications. **Done means:** update settings are checked on supported devices.
10. [ ] **Retire unsupported software.** Identify systems that no longer receive security fixes. **Done means:** each is replaced, upgraded, or isolated with a documented plan.
11. [ ] **Encrypt portable devices.** Protect laptops and phones that store or access business data. **Done means:** supported device encryption is enabled and recovery keys are controlled.
12. [ ] **Require screen locks.** Reduce exposure from unattended devices. **Done means:** devices lock automatically and staff know how to report a lost device.
13. [ ] **Remove unneeded software and extensions.** Fewer tools mean fewer accounts and update obligations. **Done means:** unused applications, plugins, and browser extensions are removed.
14. [ ] **Inventory website and online-service access.** Include the registrar, hosting, CMS, payment, and cloud tools. **Done means:** each service has a named owner and current admin list.
15. [ ] **Update the website and its components.** Apply supported updates to the CMS, themes, plugins, and integrations. **Done means:** a regular update responsibility is assigned.
16. [ ] **Confirm HTTPS and domain protection.** Keep the public site on encrypted connections and protect domain access. **Done means:** HTTPS is active and domain MFA/recovery is reviewed.
17. [ ] **Identify critical business data.** Know what must be protected and recovered first. **Done means:** important data locations and owners are documented.
18. [ ] **Limit access to sensitive data.** Give access based on business need, not convenience. **Done means:** access groups or permissions have been reviewed.
19. [ ] **Set up backups for critical data.** Include files, cloud data exports where appropriate, and key configurations. **Done means:** backup coverage and frequency are documented.
20. [ ] **Keep a separate recovery copy.** Avoid relying on a single everyday storage location. **Done means:** at least one backup is appropriately separated from primary systems.
21. [ ] **Test a restore.** Check that people can retrieve useful data, not just that a backup job says “successful.” **Done means:** a restore test and its result are recorded.
22. [ ] **Create a phishing verification rule.** Require an independent check for payment changes and unusual requests. **Done means:** staff know the verification method and use known contact details.
23. [ ] **Create a simple incident contact list.** Include decision makers, IT support, key vendors, and relevant financial contacts. **Done means:** the list is stored somewhere accessible during an email outage.
24. [ ] **Write a short incident response plan.** Describe reporting, initial containment, communication, recovery, and escalation steps. **Done means:** the responsible people can find and use it.
25. [ ] **Review the checklist regularly.** Security changes when people, systems, and business needs change. **Done means:** a recurring review date and owner are assigned.

## 10. What Should You Do First?

### Today

- Turn on MFA for business email, administrator accounts, and financial services.
- Change any known default or reused password on a critical account.
- Send the team a simple payment-change and phishing-verification rule.
- Check that a current backup exists for the data you would miss most tomorrow.

### This Week

- Build the critical-account and critical-data inventories.
- Set up or standardize a password manager.
- Review administrator access, former-user accounts, and recovery methods.
- Enable automatic updates and screen locks on supported devices.
- Perform a small restore test.

### This Month

- Document your incident contacts and short response plan.
- Review website, domain, hosting, and third-party service access.
- Address unsupported software, unnecessary tools, and missing encryption where appropriate.
- Schedule the first recurring checklist review.

## 11. When Should You Get Professional Help?

Professional IT or cybersecurity help can be worthwhile when you suspect a compromise, ransomware, fraud, unauthorized access, or exposure of sensitive data. It can also help when you need to meet contractual, legal, insurance, or industry-specific obligations; manage complex cloud systems; secure a growing workforce; or test whether your backup and recovery approach will meet business needs.

The goal is not to buy every possible control. Ask for help defining your highest risks, the responsibilities of your providers, and a realistic improvement plan.

## Conclusion

The strongest small-business security programs are usually built from consistent basics: protected accounts, safer email habits, updated systems, usable backups, and a plan for trouble. Use this checklist to make progress, then return to it as your people, tools, and obligations change.
