## The short answer

Use a business password manager to generate and store a different password for each work account, give each employee an individual login, and share only specific credentials with people who need them. Protect the manager account with a long, unique master passphrase and multi-factor authentication (MFA). Decide who owns the business vault, how access is removed, and how the company recovers before you put every account in it.

A spreadsheet may feel manageable when a team has a few logins. It becomes hard to control when people copy it, forward it, leave the business, or use it from personal devices. A password manager gives the business a purpose-built place to store and manage credentials. It does not replace access reviews, MFA on business services, or a clear offboarding process.

## What is a password manager?

A password manager is an app or service that stores login details in a protected vault. It can create long random passwords, retrieve the right one when an employee signs in, and synchronize the vault across approved devices. Many services encrypt vault data before it is synchronized, but designs and recovery models vary. Read the provider’s security and recovery documentation instead of assuming all managers work the same way.

People unlock the vault using a master passphrase, a device unlock method, or another supported sign-in method. That credential protects access to many saved logins, so it deserves special care. The manager may also use MFA to confirm sign-in to its account. MFA makes an exposed password less useful by requiring another proof, but it does not make a weak or reused master passphrase safe.

## Why avoid spreadsheets, notes, and chat messages?

These tools were not designed to manage shared business credentials across people and devices. Depending on how they are stored and shared, they can leave extra copies or give broader access than intended.

- **Spreadsheets and shared documents:** File permissions often apply to the whole document, not to each login. Copies, downloads, version history, synced devices, and old links may be difficult to track. Encryption or restricted sharing can reduce exposure, but does not provide the lifecycle and access controls built for a shared vault.
- **Plain-text notes:** Notes apps and desktop files may sync to personal accounts or devices and can be included in backups. A business may not know where every copy lives or how to revoke it.
- **Chat and email:** A password sent to a colleague can remain in message history, notifications, exports, and backups. The business may not be able to remove all copies after the task is done.
- **Reused passwords:** If one service exposes a password, an attacker may try that same value at other services. Unique passwords limit the effect of that reuse risk.

For example, a small accounting firm might keep its domain, payroll, and cloud storage logins in a spreadsheet shared with the whole team. When a contractor leaves, the owner must find every copy and determine which other accounts use those passwords. A managed vault with named users makes it easier to remove the contractor’s access and identify credentials that still need to be changed.

Use generated passwords for ordinary business logins wherever the service allows it. Do not rotate every password on an arbitrary schedule unless a service or a specific risk calls for it; prioritize evidence of exposure, role changes, and account impact.

## Choose a manager for the business

There is no single manager that fits every business. Compare how a product handles the work your team needs, and verify important claims in current provider documentation.

1. **Business ownership:** Can the company control the account, billing, administrators, and recovery if the original purchaser leaves?
2. **Named users and access groups:** Can employees sign in individually and receive access only to the folders or items their work requires?
3. **MFA and admin controls:** Does it support MFA for every user, limit administrator privileges, and let the business remove users and sessions?
4. **Sharing and auditing:** Can the team share an item without sharing the master password? Are access and administrative events visible to authorized people?
5. **Recovery and export:** What happens if an owner loses a device or leaves? Can the business recover its records safely and move them if it changes providers?
6. **Device and browser fit:** Does it work on the devices and browsers the team actually uses? Does autofill help people choose the correct saved account and website?
7. **Security evidence and support:** Review the provider’s published security design, incident communication process, vulnerability reporting, and any independent assessment. Confirm what an assessment covered; a badge alone does not prove that a service is right for the business.

Check whether the product is intended for business use and what its terms say about administrative access, employee departure, and company ownership. Avoid choosing only by price, feature count, or marketing language. Start with a small pilot and test access removal and recovery before moving all credentials.

## Create a strong master passphrase

The master passphrase should be unique to the password manager and long enough to resist guessing. A memorable sequence of several unrelated words can be easier to use than a short password with predictable substitutions. Do not base it on public facts, a familiar quotation, the company name, or another password. Do not reuse a business email password as the vault’s master passphrase.

Choose a manager that supports a sufficiently long passphrase and follow its current guidance. Store any emergency recovery material separately from the vault and restrict who can reach it. Do not put the master passphrase into a shared vault, chat, or onboarding document. NIST’s current digital identity guidance discusses password managers as a way to maintain distinct passwords and advises systems to support long passphrases and password managers.

## Protect the manager account with MFA

Turn on MFA for every manager user and especially for administrators. Prefer a phishing-resistant method such as a passkey or security key when the provider supports it and the business can maintain a backup. Otherwise, use a well-protected authenticator method supported by the service. Do not approve an unexpected sign-in request or share a one-time code with someone who contacts you.

Keep the vault’s MFA recovery codes in a business-controlled location separate from the vault itself. Decide who can access them, record when they are used, and replace them if they may have been exposed. The [MFA guide for small businesses](/guides/mfa-for-small-businesses) explains choosing and recovering MFA methods. Your wider [password policy](/guides/small-business-password-policy) should set the rules for unique passwords and account ownership across other services too.

## Keep business and personal passwords separate

Use a business-owned manager account and approved business vaults for work credentials. Employees should have individual accounts, not a single master login shared by the team. Keep personal logins in a separate personal vault so they are not mixed with company records or transferred during staff changes.

If an employee has been using a personal account to store work credentials, plan a careful move: identify the work items, transfer them into the company’s controlled vault, update ownership, and remove work access from the personal vault. Avoid importing everything without review; old duplicates and personal credentials can be accidentally copied into a business space.

## Handle shared credentials safely

First ask whether the service supports individual user accounts. Named accounts are usually easier to review and remove than a single login used by several people. If a shared login is unavoidable, store it in a restricted business vault or collection and grant access to named employees who need it.

Never share the manager’s master passphrase to let someone reach one service. Do not paste a password into chat as a shortcut. Use the manager’s supported sharing feature, limit access to the relevant people, and remove it when the task or role ends. When a shared credential is exposed or a person who knew it leaves, change the password at the service and update the vault entry. Removing a person from the vault does not necessarily change a password they already saw.

## Onboarding and offboarding employees

Treat password-manager access as part of account provisioning and removal, not as an informal handoff.

### Onboarding

- Create a named manager account using a company-controlled identity where practical.
- Require MFA and explain the master passphrase, approved devices, and reporting process.
- Grant only the vaults needed for the employee’s duties; keep administrator roles limited.
- Teach employees to verify website addresses before autofill and to report suspected exposure promptly.
- Record who approved access to sensitive or shared credentials.

### Offboarding

- Disable the employee’s manager account and revoke active sessions and devices using the provider’s documented controls.
- Remove access from email, business systems, vault groups, and any identity provider used to sign in.
- Transfer company-owned entries and notes to an authorized owner; do not ask for the employee’s master passphrase.
- Change shared passwords the employee could view when they are still needed, and update the vault.
- Review administrator access, MFA factors, recovery contacts, and any external collaborators.

Removing vault access stops future access through that account, but it cannot make a person forget a password they previously saw. Prioritize changing important shared credentials based on sensitivity and access, rather than assuming account removal changed them automatically.

## Plan emergency and recovery access

Avoid making one employee the only person who can administer or recover the business vault. Assign at least one trusted backup administrator if the service’s model supports it, protect each administrator’s account with MFA, and review that role periodically. Keep emergency procedures in a business-controlled place that remains reachable if email or the password manager is unavailable.

Understand how the provider handles a lost master passphrase, lost MFA device, employee departure, and unavailable administrator. Recovery methods differ: some restore access to an account, while others may provide an authorized business recovery process. Read what the provider can access and test the intended procedure with a non-critical account or a controlled exercise. Do not assume support can recover encrypted vault contents if the required secret has been lost.

Write down who is authorized to request recovery and how their identity will be verified. Store recovery codes or sealed emergency instructions separately from the vault, limit access, and review them when administrators change. The [cybersecurity checklist](/guides/small-business-cybersecurity-checklist) also covers high-impact accounts and business recovery planning.

## Common implementation mistakes

- Using a personal vault as the only record of company accounts.
- Sharing one manager login or master passphrase among employees.
- Giving every employee administrator or full-vault access.
- Forgetting MFA recovery codes or relying on one administrator.
- Leaving shared service passwords unchanged after a person with access departs.
- Importing old password files without checking for duplicates, stale accounts, or personal entries.
- Trusting autofill without checking the domain, especially after following an unexpected email link.
- Assuming a security badge or a provider’s recovery promise removes the need to read its design and test the company’s process.
- Failing to tell employees where to report suspected vault exposure.

## A practical rollout plan

1. **Inventory critical accounts.** Start with email, finance, payroll, domain and hosting, file storage, administrator accounts, and customer systems. Name an owner for each.
2. **Select a business-suitable manager.** Compare named-user administration, access groups, MFA, sharing, recovery, and export. Pilot with the owner and one trusted employee.
3. **Secure the manager itself.** Create individual accounts, set a strong unique master passphrase, turn on MFA, assign a backup admin, and store recovery material separately.
4. **Move high-impact accounts first.** Replace reused passwords with generated unique credentials and test sign-in and recovery. Then bring in the rest of the inventory in manageable batches.
5. **Set access rules.** Use individual accounts where possible. Put unavoidable shared logins in restricted vaults and grant access to named people only.
6. **Practice lifecycle changes.** Rehearse adding a test user, removing that user, revoking a session, and updating a shared credential. Confirm the company still controls business-owned entries.
7. **Review periodically and after changes.** Check administrators, former employees, outside support access, stale credentials, and recovery contacts when roles or services change.

For a small design studio, this might mean securing the email and domain administrator accounts first, then moving project storage and accounting logins. The studio can assign the owner as primary vault administrator and a trusted operations lead as backup, while giving designers access only to project tools. That is more manageable than importing every account at once.

## Password-manager checklist

- [ ] The business, not one employee’s personal account, owns the manager subscription and recovery path.
- [ ] Each employee has a named manager account and MFA.
- [ ] The master passphrase is long, unique, and not stored in the vault.
- [ ] Important business accounts use different generated passwords.
- [ ] Shared logins are limited to named people in restricted vaults.
- [ ] A backup administrator and separate recovery procedure are documented and tested.
- [ ] Onboarding and offboarding include vault membership, sessions, MFA, and shared credentials.
- [ ] Employees know to check the website address before autofill and where to report suspicious activity.
- [ ] The business can export or recover company-owned entries if its main administrator leaves.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should we keep one shared vault password for the whole company?

No. Give each employee an individual account and use the manager’s sharing or group controls for specific credentials. A shared master login makes it harder to know who accessed the vault and to remove one person cleanly.

### Should employees put personal passwords in the company vault?

Keep personal and business credentials separate. A business vault should hold company-owned accounts and work credentials under the business’s access and retention rules.

### Does removing an employee from the manager change shared passwords?

Usually, removing access prevents future access through that manager account; it does not change a service password the employee already viewed. Change important shared credentials when exposure or departure makes continued access inappropriate.

### What if the owner forgets the master passphrase?

Follow the recovery process you evaluated with the provider. Recovery capabilities vary, and encrypted vault data may not be recoverable without required secrets. This is why a tested backup administrator and protected recovery material matter.

## Conclusion

A business password manager works best when it is a company-controlled access process, not just a place to save passwords. Use individual accounts, unique credentials, MFA, restricted sharing, tested recovery, and clear onboarding and offboarding steps. Start with high-impact accounts, practice the lifecycle, then expand the vault as the business is ready.
