## Introduction

Business Email Compromise (BEC) is a type of payment fraud where criminals use email to trick a business into sending money or changing bank account details. Unlike typical cyberattacks, BEC rarely relies on viruses, ransomware, or malicious software attachments. Instead, it relies on deception, impersonation, and social engineering.

Consider a common scenario in a 12-person heating and plumbing contracting firm:

> On a busy Thursday afternoon, the firm's office manager receives an email from what appears to be their regular sheet-metal supplier. The email states that due to an annual banking audit, their depository account has changed. Attached is an invoice for $18,400 with the supplier's real logo, matching an active job order, but listing a new routing and account number. The email asks for payment by Friday to avoid shipping delays. 
> 
> Because the project number is genuine and the deadline is urgent, the office manager updates the vendor profile in their accounting software and schedules the wire transfer. Three days later, the real supplier calls asking why their bill has not been paid. The money was wired to an account controlled by a criminal network, and the funds have already been moved abroad.

BEC attacks succeed because they exploit everyday business habits: trust between partners, routine invoice processing, busy schedules, and reluctance to question requests from leaders or established suppliers. Attackers do not need to break through firewalls if they can convince an employee that an email is legitimate.

According to the Federal Bureau of Investigation's (FBI) Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), Business Email Compromise is one of the most financially devastating cyber threats facing organizations. In its annual reports, the IC3 recorded over $3 billion in reported BEC losses in a single year across tens of thousands of complaints, with the average incident costing over $120,000. For a small business operating on tight margins, a single diverted wire transfer can wipe out months of operating cash flow.

Preventing payment fraud does not require a large IT department. It requires a clear verification policy, basic email safeguards, and an environment where employees feel safe pausing an urgent request to verify the facts.

## What Is Business Email Compromise?

Business Email Compromise is an umbrella term for scams where attackers abuse business communication to divert funds, steal sensitive payroll records, or redirect payments. 

While ordinary phishing often casts a wide net with generic lures (such as fake delivery alerts or generic password reset links), BEC attacks are typically targeted and calculated. The attackers often research the target company, identify key personnel, and tailor their messages to mimic legitimate business operations.

Criminals execute BEC through several distinct methods:

- **Compromised Legitimate Mailboxes (Account Takeover):** The attacker gains access to a real employee's or vendor's email account using stolen passwords, credential-stuffing attacks, or adversary-in-the-middle phishing. Once inside, they monitor email threads, learn how the company conducts business, and send fraudulent payment instructions directly from the real email address.
- **Executive Impersonation (CEO Fraud):** An attacker poses as a company owner, chief executive, or senior manager. They send an urgent email to a junior finance employee or administrative assistant requesting an immediate wire transfer or confidential document, claiming they are traveling, in meetings, or otherwise unreachable by phone.
- **Vendor Email Compromise & Supply-Chain Fraud:** Rather than hacking your business directly, criminals compromise the email system of one of your suppliers or service providers. The attacker intercepts an ongoing conversation about an upcoming payment, sends a fraudulent invoice, or advises that payment must be sent to an alternate bank account.
- **Lookalike Domains (Typosquatting):** Attackers register a domain name that closely resembles a legitimate company domain—for example, replacing an `m` with `rn`, adding a hyphen, or changing a top-level domain (such as `.co` instead of `.com`). To a busy employee reading on a phone, the address appears authentic.
- **Display Name Spoofing:** Attackers use a free webmail account (like Gmail or Outlook) but set the display name to match a company executive or trusted vendor. Because mobile email applications frequently show only the display name and hide the actual email address, employees often fail to notice the sender is an external address.
- **Direct-Deposit & Payroll Diversion:** Attackers pose as an employee contacting the HR or payroll department, requesting that their upcoming paycheck direct-deposit details be switched to a new prepaid debit card or external bank account.

BEC is fundamentally an identity and authorization problem, not just a spam problem. Because many of these emails contain no malware, no links, and originate from valid mail servers, traditional antivirus and automated spam filters frequently fail to detect them.

## How a BEC Attack Usually Works

Most successful BEC attacks follow a recognizable lifecycle. Understanding this sequence helps small businesses identify intervention points where an attack can be interrupted.

```
Reconnaissance
      ↓
Impersonation or Account Compromise
      ↓
Convincing Request
      ↓
Payment or Fraudulent Action
      ↓
Fund Laundering & Attacker Disappearance
```

### 1. Reconnaissance
Attackers gather intelligence about your company using publicly available information. They check company websites, press releases, social media, and LinkedIn profiles to identify:
- Who owns the company and who manages finance, bookkeeping, or accounts payable.
- Which vendors and suppliers the company regularly works with.
- When key leaders are out of the office, traveling, or speaking at conferences.

### 2. Impersonation or Account Compromise
With this information in hand, the attacker prepares their attack vector. They might:
- Register a lookalike domain (e.g., `supplier-payments.com` instead of `supplier.com`).
- Send a phishing lure to your accounts team to harvest email login credentials.
- Infiltrate a vendor's email system and silently observe invoicing cycles for weeks.

### 3. Convincing Request
The attacker sends a carefully crafted message. They reference real project names, quote realistic invoice numbers, and match the tone and formatting of previous legitimate correspondence. 

To prevent the recipient from verifying the request, the message almost always introduces artificial urgency, confidentiality, or an explanation for why the sender cannot be reached via standard channels ("I am stepping into a board meeting," or "Our system upgrade requires payment today to avoid shipping holds").

### 4. Payment or Fraudulent Action
Trusting the request and fearing business disruption, an employee processes the payment through a wire transfer, ACH payment, or real-time payment network.

### 5. Fund Laundering & Disappearance
Once the funds arrive in the attacker's receiving account, they are immediately dispersed across multiple mule accounts, converted into cryptocurrency, or wired overseas. By the time the legitimate vendor follows up on unpaid bills, the stolen funds have moved through multiple financial institutions, making recovery difficult.

## Common BEC Warning Signs

No single warning sign proves that an email is fraudulent, and genuine business emergencies do occur. However, the presence of any of the following cues should prompt an immediate pause for independent verification:

- **Unusual Urgency:** The email demands immediate payment, threatens severe consequences if funds are not transferred within hours, or insists on completing the transaction before the end of the business day.
- **Demands for Secrecy:** The sender insists that the transaction must remain confidential, instructs the recipient not to discuss the request with colleagues, or asks to bypass standard approval channels.
- **Sudden Change in Bank Account Details:** A vendor, contractor, or landlord suddenly reports that their banking details, beneficiary name, routing number, or payment address have changed due to an "audit," "tax issue," or "bank migration."
- **Inconsistencies in the Email Address:** The display name looks familiar, but the actual email address reveals a lookalike domain, an extraneous character, a different top-level domain (`.org` instead of `.com`), or an unrelated webmail address.
- **Unusual Sender Tone or Phrasing:** The email uses greetings, signatures, or grammar that feel uncharacteristic of the supposed sender. For example, a business owner who typically writes informal, brief notes suddenly sends an overly formal, stilted request.
- **Requests to Divert from Normal Approval Procedures:** An executive or manager asks an employee to "just process it now" and promises to "sign off on the paperwork next week."
- **Inability to Speak by Phone:** The sender explicitly states they cannot take a phone call due to poor reception, back-to-back meetings, medical appointments, or travel.
- **Unusual Payment Rail or Destination:** The request asks for wire transfers to an unfamiliar out-of-state bank, an offshore institution, prepaid gift cards, or cryptocurrency.

Treat these warning signs as prompts to apply a defined verification procedure—not as an invitation to guess whether the sender is telling the truth.

## The Most Important Rule: Verify Payment Changes Independently

Technical safeguards like firewalls, spam filters, and multi-factor authentication are essential, but they cannot evaluate whether a supplier genuinely changed their bank. 

The single most effective defense against business email payment fraud is **independent, out-of-band verification**.

### What Is Out-of-Band Verification?
Out-of-band verification means confirming a request through a completely separate communication channel than the one used to deliver the request. If a request arrives by email, you do not verify it by email. You verify it using a different, pre-established channel—typically a telephone call.

### The Step-by-Step Payment Verification Procedure

Whenever an email requests a payment, a bank-detail modification, or a change in payroll direct deposit, follow this strict sequence:

1. **STOP:** Pause the transaction immediately. Do not rush to meet an artificial deadline.
2. **Do Not Reply to the Email:** Never click "Reply" or "Reply All" to confirm whether an email is genuine. If the account is compromised or spoofed, you will simply be conversing with the criminal.
3. **Contact the Requester Through a Known Channel:** Reach out to the sender using contact details you already possessed **before** the message arrived. Look up their phone number in your accounting database, a printed master contract, or an established address book. Never use a phone number, link, or contact name provided inside the suspicious email or invoice.
4. **Verbally Verify the Payment Details:** Speak directly with an authorized representative. Do not just ask, "Did you send an invoice?" An attacker may have sent a real invoice with fake bank details. Ask the contact to confirm the exact bank name, routing number, and account number.
5. **Obtain Second-Person Approval:** For transfers exceeding your business's defined risk threshold, require a second authorized manager or owner to review the verification before releasing funds.
6. **Document the Verification:** Keep a brief audit note (date, time, verified phone number used, person spoken with, and approver).
7. **Proceed or Reject:** Only when independent verbal confirmation matches your records should the accounting system be updated or payment released. If the request was fraudulent, reject the transfer and alert internal leadership immediately.

## Create a Practical Payment Verification Policy

A small business does not need a hundred-page policy manual to prevent payment fraud. A clear, one-page policy that everyone understands and follows is far more effective.

### Key Elements of a Small-Business Payment Policy

1. **Mandatory Out-of-Band Verification for Bank Changes:** Any request to change bank details, routing numbers, payee names, or payment methods must be verified verbally using a pre-established contact number. No exceptions.
2. **Dual Approval (Second-Person Sign-Off):** Payments above a defined threshold must require sign-off by two distinct individuals. 
   - *Tailor the threshold to your business:* Do not adopt an arbitrary dollar limit from another company. A solo consulting practice might set dual authorization at $1,000, while a construction contractor might set it at $5,000 or $10,000. Choose a threshold that protects your business from catastrophic loss without paralyzing daily purchasing.
3. **Urgency Does Not Override Procedure:** Write explicitly into company policy that no executive, client, or vendor deadline excuses skipping verification. Employees must know they will never be disciplined for delaying a payment to complete required security checks.
4. **Empower Staff to Pause Suspicious Transactions:** A positive, supportive security culture is crucial. If an employee feels intimidated by an executive or fears reprimand for missing a supplier's deadline, they will cut corners. Reassure staff that pausing a suspicious transfer is praiseworthy behavior.
5. **Establish Vendor Verification Protocols During Onboarding:** When you sign a contract with a new supplier, establish their designated finance contact and authorized verification telephone number up front. Inform them that your company verifies all account changes by phone.

## Protect the Email Accounts

Because many BEC attacks originate from compromised legitimate inboxes, hardening your email environment reduces an attacker's ability to gain a foothold.

### 1. Require Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)
Enable multi-factor authentication across every mailbox in your organization—not just administrators, but all staff, contractors, and shared mailboxes. 

MFA requires a second proof of identity (such as an authenticator app prompt or physical security key) in addition to a password. The [Multi-Factor Authentication Guide for Small Businesses](/guides/mfa-for-small-businesses) provides a step-by-step rollout plan.

Where your email platform supports it, deploy **phishing-resistant MFA** (such as FIDO2 security keys or passkeys). Traditional MFA methods (SMS text codes or simple mobile push approvals) can still be intercepted by modern adversary-in-the-middle (AiTM) phishing kits. Phishing-resistant credentials cryptographically bind the authentication to the legitimate login domain, stopping stolen session tokens at the gate.

### 2. Enforce Unique, Long Passwords
Never allow employees to reuse passwords across multiple services. If a non-work website is breached and an employee used their business email and password there, criminals will immediately attempt that credential against Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace.

Equip staff with a business password vault and enforce a policy of long, generated passphrases. Consult the [Small Business Password Manager Guide](/guides/small-business-password-manager-guide) and [Small Business Password Policy Guide](/guides/small-business-password-policy) for practical implementation advice.

### 3. Separate Administrator Accounts
Never use global administrator accounts for everyday tasks like reading email, browsing the web, or drafting documents. 

Create dedicated administrator accounts (e.g., `admin.jane@company.com`) that are used solely for tenant configuration, and assign everyday tasks to standard user accounts (`jane@company.com`). Restricting administrative privileges minimizes the blast radius if an individual mailbox is breached.

### 4. Audit Mailbox Forwarding and Inbox Rules
A classic tactic of BEC attackers who gain mailbox access is creating hidden inbox rules. These rules automatically redirect incoming emails from finance or suppliers to the attacker, or move legitimate messages to the "Archive" or "Trash" folder so the real account owner never sees warning emails from concerned vendors.

- Regularly audit your email tenant for external mailbox forwarding rules.
- In Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace, disable automatic forwarding to external domains by default.
- Set up administrative alerts that notify leadership whenever a new forwarding or redirect rule is created.

### 5. Revoke Active Sessions and Refresh Tokens During Incidents
Simply changing a password is not always sufficient if an account has been compromised. Modern cloud email platforms use OAuth session tokens that keep an attacker signed in on their device even after a password update. Administrators must use the email administration portal to revoke all active sign-in sessions and refresh tokens.

### 6. Secure Account Recovery Paths
Verify that email recovery phone numbers, secondary email addresses, and backup codes are controlled directly by the business. Do not allow critical administrative mailboxes to list personal Gmail addresses or former employees' cell numbers as recovery paths.

## Make Your Domain Harder to Spoof

Email was originally designed without built-in mechanisms to verify the sender's identity. To prevent attackers from sending emails that display your exact domain name in the sender field, your domain administrator should configure three standard email authentication protocols in DNS:

- **SPF (Sender Policy Framework):** A public DNS TXT record that lists the specific IP addresses and mail servers authorized to send email on behalf of your domain. Receiving mail servers check this list to verify that inbound messages come from an approved server.
- **DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail):** A cryptographic signature attached to the header of outbound emails. Your mail server signs the message with a private key, and the recipient server verifies the signature using a public key published in your DNS. This proves the email was not altered or forged during transit.
- **DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance):** A DNS record that uses SPF and DKIM to provide instructions to receiving mail servers. DMARC tells recipient servers what to do if an email claiming to be from your domain fails SPF or DKIM checks:
  - `p=none`: Monitor and send reports without blocking messages.
  - `p=quarantine`: Send failing messages to the recipient's spam or junk folder.
  - `p=reject`: Instruct receiving servers to reject and block failing messages outright.

### Critical Clarification: What Email Authentication Does and Does Not Do

It is vital not to misunderstand what SPF, DKIM, and DMARC achieve:

> **Important:** Email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) prevents unauthorized third-party servers from spoofing your exact domain name. 
> 
> It **does NOT** stop an attacker who has logged into an actual mailbox with stolen credentials. 
> It **does NOT** stop attackers who register a lookalike domain (`your-cornpany.com`) and configure their own valid SPF and DKIM records on that domain.
> It **does NOT** stop an attacker who compromises an external vendor's mailbox.

Email authentication is a necessary baseline for domain reputation and anti-spoofing, but it must be paired with account security and out-of-band payment verification to stop BEC.

## Employee Training and Culture

Security technology handles many automated threats, but payment fraud ultimately targets people. Regular, practical education is essential for building organizational resilience.

### Focus on Realistic, Short Reminders
Lengthy, once-a-year compliance lectures are quickly forgotten. Instead, provide short, 5-minute training refreshers every few months. Use realistic examples drawn from daily business routines:
- Show what a fake invoice looks like compared to a real one.
- Demonstrate how easy it is to miss a single character swap in a lookalike domain.
- Review how executive impersonation emails prey on eagerness to please senior managers.

### Create a Frictionless Reporting Process
Give employees a single, obvious way to report suspicious messages. Whether it is an email button in your mail client or a dedicated internal email address (such as `alerts@yourcompany.com`), make reporting fast and uncomplicated.

### Foster a No-Blame Reporting Culture
In payment fraud scams, time is the single most critical factor in recovering funds. If an employee realizes they mistakenly wired money to a fraudulent account, but fears they will be fired or publicly humiliated, they may hesitate or attempt to investigate privately.

Every hour of delay dramatically lowers the chance of freezing the transfer. Business leaders must cultivate a culture where employees know they will be supported when they report a mistake immediately. The message to the team must be clear: *If you suspect something went wrong, speak up immediately. Fast reporting protects the company.*

## What To Do If BEC Is Suspected: 11-Step Emergency Procedure

If you suspect a fraudulent payment was authorized, an invoice was spoofed, or a business mailbox was compromised, act immediately. Follow this numbered emergency checklist:

1. **Stop or Hold Pending Payments Immediately:** Alert internal accounting and administrative staff to immediately freeze any pending or queued outgoing payments.
2. **Contact Your Bank or Payment Provider Without Delay:** If funds were already transferred, call your bank's wire transfer or fraud department immediately. Request an urgent **Wire Recall** or ask them to initiate an **Interbank Fraud Recall / SWIFT Kill Chain**. Provide the transaction date, amount, sending account, and beneficiary account details. Every minute counts.
3. **Secure the Affected Mailbox:** Disconnect the affected computer or mobile device from the internet. Do not delete emails or folders, as they contain vital evidence.
4. **Change Compromised Credentials from a Clean Device:** Using a known clean computer or phone, reset the password for the affected mailbox and any related administrative accounts.
5. **Revoke Active Sessions and Refresh Tokens:** Sign out of all active web and mobile sessions across your email service provider to boot the attacker off the account.
6. **Inspect Mailbox Rules and Forwarding Settings:** Carefully examine the user's inbox rules, deleted items rules, and external forwarding settings. Remove any unauthorized rules created to redirect or hide incoming correspondence.
7. **Preserve Forensic Evidence:** Export and save copies of the fraudulent emails, full email headers, invoice attachments, wire confirmation receipts, and email server audit logs. These will be necessary for bank investigators, insurance adjusters, and law enforcement.
8. **Notify Internal Response Leads:** Inform the business owner, managing partner, IT support, and legal counsel in accordance with your [Cybersecurity Incident Response Plan](/guides/small-business-incident-response-plan).
9. **Contact Affected Vendors or Clients:** If a supplier's account was compromised or if an attacker used your email system to target your clients, notify the affected parties by phone so they can secure their own systems and alert their accounting teams.
10. **Follow Your Incident Response and Insurance Protocols:** Notify your commercial cyber insurance carrier if you hold a policy. Many policies require prompt reporting to cover forensic investigation costs or fraud claims.
11. **Report the Incident to Authorities:** File a formal complaint with law enforcement:
    - In the United States, file an incident report with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov). The IC3's Recovery Asset Team (RAT) specializes in coordinating with financial institutions to freeze funds if reported within 72 hours of the transfer.
    - Report business imposter scams to the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) at [reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov).
    - File a report with your local police department to obtain a formal police report number for your bank and insurer.

## Practical Small-Business BEC Checklist

Use this scannable checklist to audit your company's payment fraud safeguards:

### Payment Verification & Policies
- [ ] A written policy requires out-of-band phone verification for any bank detail or routing change.
- [ ] Employees verify bank changes using existing records, never contact details from the incoming email.
- [ ] Dual authorization is required for wire transfers and ACH payments above the company's defined threshold.
- [ ] Company policy explicitly states that perceived urgency never overrides verification procedures.
- [ ] Employees are explicitly empowered to pause suspicious payments without fear of reprimand.
- [ ] Designated finance verification contacts are established during vendor onboarding.

### Email & Account Hardening
- [ ] Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is enforced across 100% of employee email accounts.
- [ ] Phishing-resistant MFA (passkeys or hardware keys) is deployed where supported.
- [ ] Unique, complex passwords are required and managed through a business password manager.
- [ ] Daily email tasks are performed using standard user accounts, not global administrator logins.
- [ ] Automatic email forwarding to external domains is disabled by default in the tenant settings.
- [ ] Mailbox inbox rules and forwarding settings are reviewed periodically.
- [ ] Offboarding procedures immediately disable departed employees' accounts and revoke active sessions.

### Domain & Technical Defenses
- [ ] SPF records are correctly published in DNS, listing all legitimate outbound mail servers.
- [ ] DKIM signing is enabled and validated for all outgoing domain emails.
- [ ] DMARC is implemented and progressing toward an enforcement policy (`quarantine` or `reject`).
- [ ] External email warning banners are enabled to help staff identify incoming messages from external domains.

### Response Readiness
- [ ] The accounting team maintains an offline list of emergency bank fraud contacts.
- [ ] Employees know where and how to report suspicious emails within minutes.
- [ ] The business has a documented incident response plan that covers wire fraud recovery.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is business email compromise?
Business Email Compromise (BEC) is a form of cybercrime where attackers use email deception—such as compromised employee inboxes, lookalike domains, or forged executive requests—to trick a company into transferring money, paying fake invoices, or sending sensitive business data.

### Is BEC the same as phishing?
Phishing is the broad category of deceptive communications designed to trick people into clicking links, entering passwords, or downloading files. Business Email Compromise is a specialized, highly targeted form of phishing focused primarily on financial fraud and payment diversion. Many BEC attacks do not contain links or attachments; they rely entirely on convincing business correspondence.

### Can MFA prevent business email compromise?
Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is a vital defense that stops many unauthorized mailbox takeovers. However, MFA does not stop all BEC attacks. If an attacker uses a lookalike domain, poses as an executive from a personal email address, or compromises an external vendor's mailbox, your internal MFA settings will not prevent the fraudulent email from arriving. This is why technical controls must always be paired with an out-of-band payment verification policy.

### Should I trust an email from a known employee or boss?
You can trust your colleagues, but you should not treat an unverified email as authorization to bypass payment controls. Legitimate mailboxes can be compromised, and sender display names can be forged. When an email requests urgent payment, sensitive personnel files, or changes to established financial procedures, always confirm through a separate channel.

### What should I do when a supplier suddenly changes bank details?
Immediately pause any scheduled payments. Look up the supplier's phone number from your original master service contract or billing database. Call that verified number, speak directly with your established accounting contact, and read back the routing and account numbers to confirm the change verbally before updating your accounting software.

### What should I do if money was already sent?
Call your bank's fraud department immediately and request a wire recall or interbank kill chain. Time is critical: reporting within the first 24 to 72 hours gives financial institutions the best opportunity to freeze funds before they leave the correspondent banking network. Immediately afterward, file an urgent report with the FBI IC3 at [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov) and follow your incident response procedure.

## Conclusion

Business Email Compromise does not succeed because attackers possess overwhelming technical superpowers. It succeeds because criminals manipulate the human trust and routine habits that keep businesses moving every day.

Protecting a small business against payment fraud does not require expensive tools or complex technical infrastructure. It requires a balanced defense:

> **Core Defense Formula:**  
> **Account Security + Staff Awareness + Independent Verification + Incident Response**

When you protect your email accounts with multi-factor authentication, train staff to recognize urgency cues, enforce a strict out-of-band verification policy for bank changes, and maintain a tested incident plan, you effectively close the door on payment fraud.
